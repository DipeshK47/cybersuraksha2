# Class 9 Signal Studio: proposed course design

Status: approved by the user on 27 October 2026, including the course outline, Reva/Nikhil cast, shared feedback improvements and Indian English voice. Implementation in progress.

## Scope carried forward from Class 8

Replace Class 9's shared eight-mission delivery with 32 original chapters: eight each in Cybersecurity, Artificial Intelligence, Computational Thinking and Cyber Fraud. Keep English and conversational Hindi text. Supply English narration in an Indian English voice.

The actual current Grade 9 library exports two modules per subject, eight visible modules in total. Its lesson catalogue allows 11 older lesson entries. The replacement must make both delivery and access registration consistent at 32 new chapters, while retaining old IDs for historical reports and other grades.

Each chapter retains the established 40-minute lesson format: three topics and outcomes, six story scenes, an explained story decision, three practical activities, a learner evidence notebook, a reflection, a family activity and teacher guidance with a three-point rubric. Target 200–225 English caption words per story, with audio timing measured from the finished recordings. Narration is one part of the lesson.

Class 9 should advance from identifying a safe choice to testing a plan, tracing intermediate results and defending a decision with evidence. Scenarios, numerical datasets, questions, dialogue and illustrations will be newly authored. Shared underlying principles form a progression across grades.

## Cast and setting

The central characters are **Reva and Nikhil**, Class 9 students developing a teacher-supervised neighbourhood repair and learning desk. Neither name appears in the existing narrated story scripts. Reva is a patient visual thinker who sometimes trusts a neat chart too quickly. Nikhil enjoys building systems and sometimes prioritises convenience over checking assumptions. Both make mistakes, explain their reasoning and revise their work.

Their recurring settings include a tool-lending counter, a repair-request board, a small data workstation, a public notice wall and a supervised help desk. Activities use supplied fictional records. Children do not repair electrical equipment, manage real payments, collect personal datasets or investigate real accounts.

Create a distinct illustrated pair with scene expressions, rather than displaying the Class 8 character under a new name. Give the Class 9 worlds their own noticeboard, toolkit and graph motifs, with clear contrast, restrained motion and layouts that work at 390px.

## Delivery approach

Recommended: keep the tested story player, practical mechanics, activity tracking and access checks, while authoring separate Class 9 data, characters and worlds. Extract only the small shared contracts and presentation helpers needed for this course. This keeps Grade 8 regression checks useful and preserves existing teacher reporting.

A separate Class 9 lab engine would allow a free-form coding workspace, but would introduce a second validator and evidence system to maintain. This course will use constrained, testable practical controls and short Python-style traces. It does not require a browser Python interpreter.

The feedback improvement below will serve the Class 8 and Class 9 story decisions and practice labs. Class 9 receives the new cast and Indian English audio. Class 8's existing stories and recordings remain the current edition.

## Cybersecurity: eight chapters

1. **Map the repair desk's weak links.** Topics: assets and data movement; likely mistakes; matching controls to consequences. Activities: connect supplied records to the people who need them; test a sharing plan against concrete exposure checks; select a revised control set after a public link is forwarded. Evidence: a small risk-and-control map.
2. **Access that ends with the job.** Topics: named roles; temporary access; revocation and testing. Activities: configure visitor, volunteer and teacher access; run before-and-after tests when a shift ends; repair the plan when an extra helper joins for one afternoon. Evidence: a tested access ledger.
3. **Is this sign-in really yours?** Topics: intended sign-in; unexpected approval; recovery through a known route. Activities: classify supplied sign-in prompts by context; order a response to an unfamiliar session alert; choose the checks needed after a fictional device is lost. Evidence: an account-response trace.
4. **What a secure connection can promise.** Topics: connection encryption; destination identity; limits of visible trust symbols. Activities: inspect fictional hostnames and connection notices; distinguish what each observation supports; revise an upload plan on a shared network using school-approved routes. Evidence: a claim-and-evidence sheet.
5. **Collect less, keep it for less time.** Topics: purpose and minimisation; consent and audience; retention. Activities: build a minimal repair-request form; assign access and deletion points to fields; test a second use of the records against the original purpose. Evidence: a data-use card.
6. **An update needs a safe route.** Topics: trusted distribution; update timing; verification and help. Activities: separate a browser advertisement from the school's update process; order an administrator-led update check; diagnose why an urgent replacement download is unsupported by the supplied evidence. Evidence: an update decision log.
7. **When a shared folder changes.** Topics: recognising an incident; preserving useful facts; verified recovery. Activities: distinguish observations from guesses in a fictional change log; order adult-led containment and reporting; select and test a known-good backup version. Evidence: a recovery record with a stated limitation.
8. **Open the desk with evidence.** Topics: readiness checks; changed roles; remaining risk. Activities: run an integrated permission simulation; repair a failed handover; decide whether a changed opening plan has enough tested evidence. Evidence: a short readiness review, including one unresolved issue.

## Artificial Intelligence: eight chapters

1. **Choose an AI question worth measuring.** Topics: stakeholders and purpose; a measurable goal; a baseline. Activities: match needs to an appropriate problem statement; choose useful measurements without unnecessary personal fields; order a project cycle that includes reviewing the result. Evidence: a problem card with a baseline.
2. **Build the data card before the model.** Topics: provenance and permission; units and missing values; training and testing roles. Activities: repair a supplied data ledger; choose a consistent feature set; document which examples can be used and which need clarification. Evidence: a dataset card and correction record.
3. **An average can hide the queue.** Topics: mean and median; outliers; simple probability. Activities: calculate mean and median from a short wait-time list; choose which summary answers a stated question; calculate a probability from a small, explicit set of coloured tickets. Evidence: calculations with an explanation of the denominator.
4. **Predict, then count the mistakes.** Topics: predictions and labels; false alarms and misses; the cost of a decision. Activities: train and run the visible classroom model; fill a small results table from its actual predictions; compare two supplied settings for a stated purpose. Evidence: a model test record. No score alone establishes that a model is safe for every use.
5. **Pixels are data, not understanding.** Topics: pixel values; useful visual features; changes in the input. Activities: encode a supplied brightness value in binary; mark a feature in a small grid; test whether a stated rule survives a changed image. Evidence: a feature record that separates observed pixels from an interpretation.
6. **Words need context.** Topics: ambiguity; language models and generated text; source-based checking. Activities: classify the same word in different supplied sentences; identify claims a generated answer cannot support from its reference sheet; choose a useful human-review step when the information is absent. Evidence: an annotated answer audit.
7. **Whose mistakes cost more?** Topics: group denominators; uneven errors; review and appeal. Activities: calculate error rates for two small groups; identify missing evidence before a fairness claim; match disagreement cases to a suitable human review. Evidence: a group-results sheet with sample-size limits.
8. **Pilot the repair-request sorter.** Topics: project-cycle revision; bounded deployment; monitoring and stopping. Activities: revise a training selection without copying held-out labels into it; test the prototype on supplied requests; approve, revise or stop a pilot using explicit criteria. Evidence: a prototype review and human fallback plan. The sorter routes fictional request categories and does not diagnose equipment.

## Computational Thinking: eight chapters

1. **Turn a request into a specification.** Topics: inputs and outputs; constraints; test cases. Activities: classify the parts of a tool-lending request; calculate whether a proposed kit meets capacity limits; select tests that expose an ambiguous requirement. Evidence: a testable specification.
2. **Trace the values, not the guess.** Topics: variables and assignment; numeric and text values; expression order. Activities: trace a short Python-style calculation; repair a mismatch between a count and a text label; work out the changed result when an input changes. Evidence: a line-by-line value trace.
3. **Conditions must cover the edges.** Topics: AND, OR and NOT; inclusive boundaries; branch priority. Activities: complete a decision table; test values on either side of a capacity boundary; repair a condition that sends one request down two conflicting branches. Evidence: a boundary-test sheet.
4. **Keep records together.** Topics: lists and positions; stable identifiers; updates and queues. Activities: reconnect supplied IDs to the right records; trace a queue after removal and insertion; identify an update that changes the wrong person's row. Evidence: a before-and-after record trace using invented people.
5. **Give a reusable step clear inputs.** Topics: parameters; returned values; independent calls. Activities: calculate outputs of a small kit-count function; order a caller/function/return trace; choose tests that reveal a hidden dependency on an earlier call. Evidence: a reusable-step test card.
6. **Search without skipping a record.** Topics: bounded loops; comparison counts; search preconditions. Activities: trace a search through a short list; count comparisons for found and missing items; choose a method only when its stated ordering requirements hold. Evidence: a search trace, including an empty-list case.
7. **The shortest route is not always cheapest.** Topics: movement and cost; obstacles; a resource budget. Activities: compare two explicit routes; build and run a route on a small weighted grid; repair it after a path cost changes. Evidence: the actual route, visited cells, total cost and budget check. Repeated visits cost again; the starting cell is free. Equal-cost valid routes can both pass.
8. **Debug the dispatch plan.** Topics: dependencies; intermediate state; retesting after change. Activities: repair a dispatch sequence; calculate the resulting queue/capacity; run a revised route after a closure. Evidence: a debugging record showing the original fault and the retest. The learner can complete this from supplied traces without writing executable Python.

## Cyber Fraud: eight chapters

1. **The scholarship form that asks too much.** Topics: claimed purpose; excessive information; independent verification. Activities: inspect a fictional form; match requested fields to a justified purpose; order a safe teacher-assisted verification plan. Evidence: a request audit.
2. **A tiny task, then a deposit.** Topics: escalating commitments; displayed balances; pressure to pay. Activities: trace an offer from a small reward to a larger demand; distinguish verified transfers from a claimed screen balance; reject and explain the next unsafe step. Evidence: a pressure-chain map.
3. **The parcel-fee detour.** Topics: unexpected contact; destination changes; known support routes. Activities: compare a message with a supplied order record; inspect the real destination in a fictional URL; choose a response that does not use the message's own support contact. Evidence: a verification trail.
4. **The refund that starts a payment.** Topics: payment direction; payee and amount; status versus a promise. Activities: read a fictional merchant-payment approval; trace the resulting balance change; distinguish a genuine incoming refund record from a request to authorise a new outgoing payment. Evidence: a payment-direction ledger. No real payment app is opened.
5. **When your account becomes somebody else's route.** Topics: account lending; moving money for others; getting help. Activities: inspect an invented transfer chain; identify the request to share or operate an account; build a calm adult-assisted response without forwarding money. Evidence: a risk map and help plan. The exercise does not teach transfer evasion.
6. **The subscription inside the free trial.** Topics: recurring authorisation; timing and total cost; revocation and confirmation. Activities: inspect supplied trial terms; calculate a clearly stated recurring charge; test whether a cancellation or authorisation change is actually confirmed in the fictional records. Evidence: a recurring-payment checklist.
7. **Trace the appeal to its source.** Topics: emotional pressure; identity and purpose; limits of a copied picture. Activities: distinguish an appeal's claims from verifiable information; choose independent sources; prepare a privacy-respecting request for adult review. Evidence: a claim-and-source note. A missing fact alone is not proof of fraud.
8. **Build a case note that helps.** Topics: factual timelines; preserving evidence privately; supporting the affected person. Activities: put supplied events in order; remove unnecessary personal details from a report; match the situation to prompt teacher/adult and provider support. Evidence: a concise case note with known facts, uncertainties and next actions. Recovery is not guaranteed.

## Clearer feedback in the lesson and practice lab

Checking must evaluate the submitted values, not the translated labels or the appearance of the controls. After a checked wrong answer, show an explicit, respectful result close to the task:

- **You chose:** the learner's actual option, entered value, selected items or submitted plan.
- **This answer is incorrect because:** an explanation tied to the supplied case and that exact mismatch.
- **What to reconsider:** a useful next check or reasoning step. Keep the current answer available to edit.

For multiple selection, show every wrongly selected item and every missing required item separately, with reasons. Mark selections that are already supported as supported; do not describe an entire partial answer as wrong in every part. For matching, show each mismatched row and its reason. Ordering, numerical fields and simulations must identify the first failing relationship, calculation or test result rather than return only a generic retry sentence.

Routes report the submitted path, failure move or missed condition, and actual cost. Models report the actual prediction and expected label for each test, including an uncertain/tied result. Permission tests show the feature or resource that is wrongly exposed or unavailable. Feedback uses words and icons as well as colour and is announced through a live region.

If the learner changes an answer, hide or clearly invalidate the previous result until they check again. Do not show a stale explanation as though it describes the edited answer. Empty/unfilled controls receive a completion prompt before a scored submission; they do not silently use up a first response. A nonempty, complete but wrong submission remains a recorded wrong first response. A corrected retry can unlock progress without replacing that first-response score.

Keep the learner's actual submitted answer and the diagnostic evidence in teacher history, with readable labels and bounded payload chunks. Successful work remains in the bilingual notebook after advancing and reloading. Existing authentication, school isolation and completion requirements remain in force.

### Worked wrong-answer example

Task: Reva leaves the repair desk after a temporary shift. Which access plan is ready for tomorrow?

Selected option: **Keep her edit link active in case she returns.**

English result:

> You chose: Keep her edit link active in case she returns.
>
> This choice is incorrect. Her shift has ended, but this link still lets her change tomorrow's timetable. A possible future visit does not justify access now.
>
> Reconsider: end the temporary access, then test whether the former helper can still edit. The public notice can remain readable.

Hindi result:

> आपने चुना: शायद वह लौट आए, इसलिए उसका एडिट लिंक चालू रखें।
>
> यह चुनाव गलत है। उसकी शिफ़्ट खत्म हो गई है, फिर भी इस लिंक से वह कल की समय-सारणी बदल सकती है। भविष्य में लौटने की संभावना अभी एक्सेस रखने का कारण नहीं है।
>
> दोबारा सोचें: अस्थायी एक्सेस खत्म करें, फिर जाँचें कि पुरानी हेल्पर अब भी एडिट कर सकती है या नहीं। पब्लिक नोटिस पढ़ने की सुविधा बनी रह सकती है।

## Six-scene bilingual sample: access that ends with the job

The following captions illustrate the expected length and teaching style. The decision is in scene 4; its consequence follows in scene 5.

### 1. One convenient folder

Reva and Nikhil are opening a neighbourhood repair desk with their teacher. Volunteers need a shared timetable, a request list and a public notice. Nikhil creates one folder and gives everybody the same editing link.

रेवा और निखिल अपनी टीचर के साथ मोहल्ले का रिपेयर डेस्क शुरू कर रहे हैं। वॉलंटियरों को साझा समय-सारणी, अनुरोधों की सूची और पब्लिक नोटिस चाहिए। निखिल एक फ़ोल्डर बनाकर सबको एक ही एडिट लिंक दे देता है।

### 2. The role changed

At first, one link seems convenient. Then a visitor opens the private request sheet, and a former volunteer can still change tomorrow's timetable. Nobody stole a password. The access plan allowed more than each role needed.

शुरू में एक लिंक सुविधाजनक लगता है। फिर एक विज़िटर निजी अनुरोधों वाली शीट खोल लेता है और पहले काम कर चुका वॉलंटियर अब भी कल की समय-सारणी बदल सकता है। किसी ने पासवर्ड नहीं चुराया। एक्सेस प्लान ने हर भूमिका की ज़रूरत से ज़्यादा अनुमति दी थी।

### 3. Separate the purposes

Reva separates the public notice from working documents. Visitors can read the notice. Current volunteers can update the timetable. Only the responsible teacher can open private contact details. Temporary helpers lose their access when their shift ends.

रेवा पब्लिक नोटिस को कामकाजी दस्तावेज़ों से अलग करती है। विज़िटर नोटिस पढ़ सकते हैं। मौजूदा वॉलंटियर समय-सारणी बदल सकते हैं। निजी कॉन्टैक्ट जानकारी केवल ज़िम्मेदार टीचर खोल सकती हैं। अस्थायी हेल्परों का एक्सेस शिफ़्ट खत्म होते ही समाप्त हो जाता है।

### 4. Choose a plan to test

The team must choose how to repair the plan. Making the whole folder public keeps the problem. Giving everyone a new shared password also mixes roles. Separate named access, then test it as each person before opening the desk.

टीम को प्लान सुधारने का तरीका चुनना है। पूरा फ़ोल्डर पब्लिक करने से समस्या बनी रहती है। सबको नया साझा पासवर्ड देने से भी अलग भूमिकाएँ मिल जाती हैं। हर व्यक्ति के नाम से ज़रूरत के अनुसार एक्सेस तय करें, फिर डेस्क खोलने से पहले हर भूमिका में उसे जाँचें।

### 5. Look at the result

In the simulator, a visitor sees the notice but cannot open contacts. A former helper's edit fails. A current volunteer updates a timetable entry. The teacher records these results, because choosing permissions is only the start of checking them.

सिमुलेटर में विज़िटर नोटिस देख पाता है, लेकिन कॉन्टैक्ट जानकारी नहीं खोल पाता। पहले काम कर चुकी हेल्पर का एडिट नहीं होता। मौजूदा वॉलंटियर समय-सारणी की एक एंट्री बदल पाता है। टीचर ये नतीजे दर्ज करती हैं, क्योंकि अनुमति चुनना उसकी जाँच की शुरुआत भर है।

### 6. One afternoon, not forever

Now an extra helper joins for one afternoon. Decide what that helper needs, set an end time and test the next day's access. Keep a record showing which actions worked, which were blocked and why.

अब एक और हेल्पर केवल एक दोपहर के लिए जुड़ता है। तय करें कि उसे क्या चाहिए, एक्सेस खत्म होने का समय रखें और अगले दिन का एक्सेस जाँचें। दर्ज करें कि कौन-से काम हुए, कौन-से रुके और क्यों।
